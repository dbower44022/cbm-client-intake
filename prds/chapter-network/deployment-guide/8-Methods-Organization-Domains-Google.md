# New Chapter Deployment Guide — Methods for the Organization, the Domain Names and Google Workspace

**Document:** The written-out steps for four stages — setting up the chapter as a
legal organization (stage 1), signing the agreement with the central support
organization (stage 2), registering the chapter's domain names (stage 3), and
setting up Google Workspace and the chapter's email (stage 4)
**Version:** 0.5
**Status:** Draft for review
**Owner:** Doug Bower
**Last Updated:** 09-18-26 14:45

---

## What this is

The first four stages of the eighteen. They build the organization and the accounts
everything else sits on. None of them has ever been done for a chapter, and no
practice system can test them, because they are about an organization rather than a
computer. Every step below is marked **not yet tried**.

They are written from the settled rulings for the chapter network
(`prds/chapter-network/DECISIONS.md`), the governance design
(`governance-and-exit.md`), the chapter information form (`chapter-values.md`), and
what Cleveland's own Google Workspace taught the project the hard way.

**Some of these steps are outside what the central support organization knows.**
Setting up a nonprofit corporation, getting tax status and opening a bank account
are legal and financial work. The steps are still written down, because a chapter
that does not know they exist gets stuck. For each one, the step says what it must
produce, when to start it, and what waits on it. It then says plainly that the
chapter gets the actual advice elsewhere, from a lawyer, an accountant or its state's
nonprofit association. Where a step names a waiting time, it is a typical time, not
a promise.

Each step carries the same eight headings as the other methods documents.

**Two open questions from the plan run through these stages** and are not answered
here: whether a chapter needs one domain name or two, and what happens to account
ownership when the chapter works under a sponsoring nonprofit. Steps that depend on
them name the question.

# Stage 1 — Set up the chapter as a legal organization

**Stage status: not yet tried.** The central support organization does not do this
work. It is in the guide because two later stages depend on it: the nonprofit
discount for Google Workspace (step 4.13) and the nonprofit hosting credits
(step 5.3) both need the chapter's nonprofit evidence.

**Start this stage first and start it early.** Getting nonprofit tax status is the
longest wait in the whole guide, and everything a chapter pays for before it arrives
is paid at full price.

---

### 1.1 Choose the chapter's name

**Done when:** the founding group has agreed one name, a search of state business
registrations and trademarks shows no conflict, and the matching domain names are
still available to buy.

**Who:** the chapter.

**First:** nothing.

**How to do it today:** agree the name as a group. Then do three searches:

- the state's business registration search, run by the Secretary of State's office
  in most states;
- the federal trademark search, run by the United States Patent and Trademark
  Office;
- a domain registrar's search, for the domain names the chapter would want. See
  step 3.2 for the open question about whether a chapter needs one or two.

Write the name exactly as it will appear everywhere, including capital letters.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** all three searches come back clear, and the results are
saved with the date.

**What goes wrong:** changing the name later. The name appears on every page the
software shows, in the CRM, in email sending names and in the legal filing. The
software can change it in one setting, but the legal filing, the domain names and
the bank account cannot be changed so easily. Choose once.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 1.2 Incorporate the chapter

**Done when:** the state has accepted the articles of incorporation and returned a
stamped copy.

**Who:** the chapter, with its own legal advice.

**First:** step 1.1.

**How to do it today:** file articles of incorporation for a nonprofit corporation
with the state. The chapter gets the advice on how to do that elsewhere: from a
lawyer, or from its state's nonprofit association, which often publishes a guide.
The articles usually need wording about purpose and about what happens to assets if
the organization closes. The tax authority looks for that wording later (step 1.5),
so it is worth getting right the first time.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged. This stays outside the software.

**How you know it worked:** the stamped articles are in the chapter's files.

**What goes wrong:** articles written without the wording the tax authority expects.
Fixing them means filing an amendment, which delays step 1.5.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 1.3 Get a federal employer identification number

**Done when:** the number has been issued and the confirmation letter is filed.

**Who:** the chapter.

**First:** step 1.2.

**How to do it today:** apply to the Internal Revenue Service. Applying online is
free, and the number is usually issued at the end of the online session. The
application asks for a responsible person, who must be an officer of the chapter.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the confirmation letter from the Internal Revenue
Service is saved in the chapter's files.

**What goes wrong:** paying a third-party website to apply. Several charge a fee for
a free service.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 1.4 Appoint a board and adopt bylaws

**Done when:** bylaws are adopted, directors are named, and the minutes record which
officers may sign contracts.

**Who:** the chapter, with its own legal advice.

**First:** step 1.2.

**How to do it today:** hold the first board meeting. Adopt bylaws, elect officers,
and minute which officers may sign contracts and open accounts. The agreement with
the central support organization (step 2.6) and the bank account (step 1.6) both
need that minute.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** signed minutes of the first meeting are in the chapter's
files.

**What goes wrong:** minutes that do not name who may sign. The bank and the central
support organization will both ask.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 1.5 Obtain nonprofit tax status, or a sponsorship arrangement

**Done when:** either the determination letter from the tax authority has arrived, or a
signed sponsorship agreement is in place naming the sponsoring nonprofit.

**Who:** the chapter, with its own legal or accounting advice.

**First:** steps 1.3 and 1.4.

**How to do it today:** there are two routes. The chapter gets the advice on which
one suits it elsewhere.

**Its own status.** Apply to the Internal Revenue Service for recognition as a
charity under section 501(c)(3). Small organizations may qualify for the shorter
Form 1023-EZ; others file the full Form 1023. The short form is typically answered
within weeks to a few months. The full form typically takes several months or
longer.

**A sponsoring nonprofit.** An existing charity agrees to take the chapter in under
its own status, usually called fiscal sponsorship. This is faster, but read the
warning below before choosing it.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** either the determination letter or the signed sponsorship
agreement is in the chapter's files.

**What goes wrong:** choosing the sponsorship route without seeing what it does to
the accounts. The nonprofit discounts for Google Workspace (step 4.13) and for
hosting (step 5.3) are claimed against a nonprofit's own status. A sponsored chapter
has none of its own, so those accounts may have to sit under the sponsor. That cuts
against the settled rule that each chapter owns its own accounts and can withdraw
access at any time. This is an open question in the plan, and nothing yet says how
to handle it. A chapter choosing sponsorship should raise it with the central
support organization before step 4.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 1.6 Open a bank account

**Done when:** the account is open and at least two officers can authorise a payment.

**Who:** the chapter.

**First:** steps 1.3 and 1.4.

**How to do it today:** take the stamped articles, the employer identification number
letter and the board minute naming the signers to the bank. Banks set their own list
of documents, so ask first. The chapter gets banking advice elsewhere.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** two officers can each see and approve a payment.

**What goes wrong:** one person as the only signer. If that person leaves, nobody can
pay the domain name renewal, the Google Workspace bill or the hosting bill.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 1.7 Name the person who acts for the chapter during setup

**Done when:** one named person is recorded as the chapter's main contact for the whole
setup, with authority to open accounts and grant access to others.

**Who:** the chapter's board.

**First:** step 1.4.

**How to do it today:** the board minutes the name, and the authority: to open
accounts in the chapter's name, and to grant the central support organization access
to them.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the minute exists, and the central support organization
has a copy.

**What goes wrong:** a setup contact who is also the only person holding the
accounts. Stages 3 and 4 each end with a step that removes the founder's personal
hold on an account, for exactly this reason.

**Status:** not yet tried.

# Stage 2 — Sign the agreement with the central support organization

**Stage status: not yet tried.** A draft of the standard agreement exists and is in
review (Doug, 09-18-26). It is not held in this repository. The steps below are
written from the settled rulings and the governance design. Once the agreement is
final, check these steps against it clause by clause.

---

### 2.1 Agree what the central support organization does

**Done when:** the chapter has in writing the list of what is provided, what is not,
and how quickly ordinary requests are answered.

**Who:** both.

**First:** step 1.7.

**How to do it today:** set out three lists. What is provided: building and running
the CRM system and the applications, the weekly software release, and the support
answers. What is not provided: the chapter's legal, tax and banking work, its
website's content, and its own training beyond the first sessions. And the answer
times. The governance design separates two kinds of request. Ordinary requests,
such as adding a user or resetting a password, get a published answer time. Requests
to change the software go to the decision forum instead. The first kind must never
wait behind the second.

**How it will be done later:** a standard schedule attached to every chapter's
agreement.

**How you know it worked:** the chapter can answer, from the document, "who do I ask
to add a new staff member, and how long will it take?"

**What goes wrong:** slow answers to ordinary requests. The governance design names
this as the way the whole arrangement fails: a chapter that waits too long asks for
its own administrator account, and granting one ends the identical-software rule.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 2.2 Agree the cost

**Done when:** the fee is agreed in writing, and it is clear which costs the chapter
pays directly to other companies rather than through the fee.

**Who:** both.

**First:** step 2.1.

**How to do it today:** the chapter pays its own hosting, Google Workspace and domain
names directly, in its own accounts. That follows from the settled rule that each
chapter owns its own accounts. It is proposed, not yet ruled, that the fee then
covers labour only. List every direct cost with its supplier and its approximate
price, so the chapter's budget has no surprises.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the chapter's treasurer can name every recurring cost and
who it is paid to.

**What goes wrong:** the add-on products for the CRM. The permission roles depend on
two paid add-on products, which a chapter would have to license. Whether they are
part of the standard is an open question. Settle it before quoting a cost.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 2.3 Accept the identical-software rule

**Done when:** the chapter's board has been told in writing that no chapter gets its
own custom fields, form questions or list choices, and has been shown how to ask for
a change that would apply to every chapter.

**Who:** the central support organization explains; the chapter's board accepts.

**First:** step 2.1.

**How to do it today:** give the board the rule in the settled rulings' own words:
core or nothing. A change a chapter wants either becomes part of the software for
every chapter, or it does not happen. There is no third answer. Show the board where
change requests go and when the decision forum meets.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the board minutes record that the rule was read and
accepted.

**What goes wrong:** a promise made in a sales conversation that one field can be
added "just for you". The first exception ends the rule for everyone.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 2.4 Agree the access the central support organization will hold

**Done when:** it is written down which of the chapter's accounts the central support
organization will administer, and that the chapter may withdraw that access at any
time.

**Who:** both.

**First:** step 2.1.

**How to do it today:** list the accounts: the hosting account, the Google Workspace
administrator account, the video meeting account if there is one, and the CRM system.
State the settled rule that the central support organization holds the only CRM
administrator accounts, and that chapter staff hold ordinary accounts. State the
other half of the same rule too: the chapter can always get in through the server it
owns, and can withdraw the central support organization's access at any time.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the list is in the agreement, and each account on it has
a named step later in this guide that grants the access.

**What goes wrong:** promising the chapter emergency access with no written way to
use it. The emergency access procedure is on the work list (item 5).

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 2.5 Agree the leaving terms

**Done when:** the notice period, the wind-down, and the list of everything the chapter
takes with it are written into the agreement.

**Who:** both.

**First:** step 2.4.

**How to do it today:** write in the terms the governance design requires:

- a notice period;
- a defined wind-down;
- an exit kit: a copy of the CRM's database, an export of the application's own
  database, a transfer of the chapter's documents in Google Drive, and the chapter's
  own files such as its logo and colour file;
- a perpetual licence to the last version of the software the chapter received.

Also write in the rule that the central support organization may stop working for a
chapter that stops paying, but can never lock it out. The chapter's system keeps
running. It slowly falls behind on security updates over the following months.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** each item on the exit kit list names what it is and who
produces it.

**What goes wrong:** leaving out the application's own database. Some of the
chapter's records live only there, not in the CRM: the partner and funder discussion
notes, the full history of every submission, and any analytics pages the chapter
built. "You keep your CRM" is not the same as "you keep your data".

**Status:** not yet tried. The exit has never been rehearsed either.

---

### 2.6 Sign the agreement

**Done when:** both sides have signed and each holds a copy.

**Who:** both.

**First:** steps 2.1 to 2.5, and the board minute naming who may sign (step 1.4).

**How to do it today:** sign, and store the chapter's copy where more than one
officer can reach it.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** both copies are signed and dated.

**What goes wrong:** nothing known yet.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 2.7 Set up the chapter's password vault

**Done when:** the chapter owns a Proton Pass business organization with at least two
chapter owners, and at least two named people from the central support organization
are members of its shared operations vault (ruled 09-18-26).

**Who:** the chapter owns it; the central support organization sets it up alongside.

**First:** the agreement is signed (step 2.6).

**How to do it today:** the chapter buys a Proton Pass business plan in its own name
and pays for it itself. Two chapter officers are made owners. Create two vaults:

- **Operations**, shared with the central support organization's named people.
  Every sign-in, recovery code and machine secret the system runs on goes here.
- **Board**, kept to the chapter, for anything the central support organization has
  no need to see.

Every account created from stage 3 onward is recorded in the Operations vault the
moment it is created, not gathered up afterwards.

Two rules go with the vault. Use named sign-ins wherever a system allows them:
Google Workspace, the hosting account, the domain registrar and the CRM all support
individual accounts with two-step sign-in, and each person uses their own. The vault
holds only what cannot belong to one person: the break-glass top-level sign-ins,
two-step recovery codes and machine secrets.

**How it will be done later:** the settings generator reads the machine secrets
straight from the vault with Proton Pass's command-line tool, so no secret is ever
written to a file.

**How you know it worked:** a second chapter owner and a second central member each
sign in and open the Operations vault without help.

**What goes wrong:** a vault owned by the central support organization instead of the
chapter. The chapter could then lose access to its own credentials in a dispute,
which breaks the rule that neither side can lock the other out.

**Status:** not yet tried. Proton Pass chosen on 09-18-26.

# Stage 3 — Register the chapter's domain names

**Stage status: not yet tried.** The registration itself is ordinary. What matters is
who controls the account the domain names sit in, because losing that account loses
the chapter's email and website together.

---

### 3.1 Choose the founding email address used for setup

**Done when:** one named person's existing working email address is recorded as the
address that will create the first accounts, and it is understood that this is
temporary.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact (step 1.7).

**First:** step 1.7.

**How to do it today:** use the setup contact's own existing email address. The
chapter has no email address of its own yet, because the chapter's mailboxes are
created in stage 4, on the domain name registered in this stage. Write down that the
address is temporary, and that step 4.14 replaces it.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the address and the person's name are written down.

**What goes wrong:** forgetting the address is temporary. The domain name then stays
tied to one volunteer's personal email for years.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 3.2 Decide how many domain names the chapter needs, and choose them

**Done when:** the names are chosen and confirmed available. See the open question in
the plan document about whether a chapter needs one domain name or two.

**Who:** the chapter, advised by the central support organization.

**First:** step 1.1.

**How to do it today:** this depends on an open question. Cleveland uses two domain
names: one for the public website, and a second, shorter one for email addresses and
mentor logins. A new chapter could copy that, or use one domain name for everything.
Two is more to explain and more to pay for. One is simpler, but ties the chapter's
email addresses to its marketing name for good. Until the question is ruled, explain
both to the chapter and record which it chose.

The chapter's CRM and applications each need their own web address, but these are
names under a domain name the chapter already owns, such as `crm.` and `apps.`, not
new domain names.

**How it will be done later:** the plan's open question is ruled, and this step
states the answer.

**How you know it worked:** the chosen names are written down, and a registrar's
search shows each one available.

**What goes wrong:** choosing a long email domain name. Every mentor's address is
their first and last name at that domain name, so a long domain name makes long
addresses.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 3.3 Create the domain registrar account

**Done when:** the account exists, is in the chapter's name, and uses the founding
email address.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact.

**First:** steps 3.1 and 3.2.

**How to do it today:** open an account at an ordinary domain registrar. Put the
chapter's legal name as the account holder, not the setup contact's own name. Use
the founding email address from step 3.1.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the registrar's account page shows the chapter's legal
name.

**What goes wrong:** an account in a person's own name. If that person leaves, the
registrar may refuse to hand the domain names to the chapter.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 3.4 Register and pay for the domain names

**Done when:** a public ownership lookup returns the chapter as the registrant, and the
registration runs at least a year ahead.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact.

**First:** step 3.3, and the bank account (step 1.6).

**How to do it today:** register each domain name chosen in step 3.2, for at least a
year. Pay with the chapter's own payment card, not a personal one.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** a public ownership lookup for each domain name shows the
chapter as the registrant. Many registrars hide the registrant's details behind a
privacy service. In that case the registrar's own account page is the check.

**What goes wrong:** paying with a personal card. When that card expires, the renewal
fails, and nobody at the chapter is told.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 3.5 Turn on automatic renewal

**Done when:** every domain name is set to renew automatically and a working payment
method is on file.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact.

**First:** step 3.4.

**How to do it today:** in the registrar account, turn on automatic renewal for every
domain name. Check the payment card's expiry date.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** each domain name shows automatic renewal as on.

**What goes wrong:** a lapsed domain name. The chapter's email and website stop
together, and so do the CRM and applications if their web addresses are under that
domain name.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 3.6 Turn on two-step sign-in for the registrar account

**Done when:** signing in requires a second factor, and the recovery codes are stored
where more than one person can reach them.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact.

**First:** step 3.3.

**How to do it today:** turn on two-step sign-in in the registrar account. Store the
recovery codes in the chapter's vault (step 2.7), where at least two officers can
reach them.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** signing in asks for the second step.

**What goes wrong:** the second step tied to one person's phone, and the recovery
codes held by the same person.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 3.7 Move the domain names' DNS to the chapter's Cloudflare account

**Done when:** each domain name is a zone in a Cloudflare account the chapter owns, the
registrar points at Cloudflare's name servers, two-step sign-in is on, the central
support organization's named people are members, and the sign-in and recovery codes
are in the chapter's vault. CRMBuilder supports no other DNS provider.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact, with the central support organization.

**First:** steps 3.4 and 3.6, and the chapter's vault (step 2.7).

**How to do it today:**

1. Create a Cloudflare account in the chapter's name, signed up with a chapter
   address rather than a personal one. The free plan is enough.
2. Add each domain name to the account. Cloudflare reads the existing records and
   gives two name servers.
3. In the registrar account, replace the name servers with Cloudflare's two.
4. Turn on two-step sign-in. Put the sign-in and recovery codes in the chapter's
   vault.
5. Invite the central support organization's named people as members.

From this point on, every DNS record is made in Cloudflare, not at the registrar.
That includes Google's verification and mail records (steps 4.3 and 4.4), the CRM's
address (step 9.5) and the application's address (step 11.10).

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** Cloudflare shows each zone as active, and a name-server
lookup on the domain returns Cloudflare's two servers.

**What goes wrong:** turning on Cloudflare's proxy for the CRM or the application.
Both addresses must be "DNS only", with the grey cloud. Their certificates are issued
by a direct check against the server, and the proxy blocks that check, so the
certificate fails to issue or to renew. The public website can use the proxy;
Cleveland's does.

**Status:** not yet tried. Cleveland's two domains and the trial chapter's domain are
all on Cloudflare this way.

# Stage 4 — Set up Google Workspace and the chapter's email

**Stage status: not yet tried.** Cleveland runs its own Google Workspace, and several
of the rules below were learned from it. No chapter has done this stage.

Google Workspace is Google's paid service for an organization's email, calendars and
documents. The software reads and sends mail, creates calendar events and stores
documents through the chapter's Google Workspace. So the mailboxes created here are
not only for people: several are used by the software, and those have rules of their
own.

---

### 4.1 Choose the Google Workspace branch

> **Superseded 09-18-26.** Every chapter hosts its own email on its own Google
> Workspace; the central support organization does not provide one. The step is now
> "Confirm the chapter will hold its own Google Workspace" — see `steps/stage-04.yaml`.

**Done when:** the chapter has chosen either to hold its own Google Workspace or to
have one provided by the central support organization, the choice is recorded, and
the board has seen in writing what the provided option means if the chapter later
leaves.

**Who:** the chapter's board, advised by the central support organization.

**First:** stage 2.

**How to do it today:** the settled rulings allow both branches. In the first, the
chapter holds its own Google Workspace and grants the software's machine account
permission inside it. In the second, the central support organization provides the
chapter a domain inside a Google Workspace the central support organization holds.
It is proposed, not ruled, that every chapter that can should take the first branch,
and the second should be a stage on the way to the first.

Before the board chooses, give it in writing what the second branch means on
leaving. The chapter's mail, documents and calendars would sit in an account the
central support organization controls. They can be moved out, but it is real work,
and the governance design calls this the only hard exit in the whole arrangement.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the board minutes record the choice, and the written
explanation.

**What goes wrong:** choosing the second branch for speed without reading the
consequence. Nothing in the software differs between the branches, so nothing will
warn anyone later.

**Status:** not yet tried. The rest of this stage is written for the first branch,
which is the proposed default. The second branch has no written steps yet.

---

### 4.2 Create the Google Workspace account

**Done when:** the account exists on the chapter's email domain and the first
administrator can sign in.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact.

**First:** step 4.1, and the email domain name registered (step 3.4).

**How to do it today:** sign up for Google Workspace using the chapter's email domain
name. Google asks for a first administrator. That first administrator becomes the
chapter's own administrator account in step 4.5, so use the setup contact's name on a
chapter address, not a role name.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the setup contact signs in to the Google Workspace admin
console.

**What goes wrong:** signing up with the website domain name when the chapter chose a
separate email domain name in step 3.2. The mailboxes then land on the wrong domain
name. Check step 3.2's record first.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 4.3 Verify the domain

**Done when:** Google reports the domain as verified.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact, with the central support organization's help.

**First:** step 4.2.

**How to do it today:** Google gives a verification record to add to the domain's
DNS. Add it in the chapter's Cloudflare account (step 3.7), not at the registrar. Then return to the Google
Workspace admin console and ask it to check.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the admin console shows the domain as verified.

**What goes wrong:** impatience. A new DNS record can take from minutes
to a day to be seen. Wait before assuming the record is wrong.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 4.4 Switch mail delivery to Google

**Done when:** a message sent from outside to an address on the domain arrives in the
Google mailbox.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact, with the central support organization's help.

**First:** step 4.3.

**How to do it today:** Google gives the mail delivery records. Set them in the
chapter's Cloudflare account (step 3.7), replacing any mail records already there.
Mail records are never proxied; Cloudflare shows them as DNS only by itself. Also add the records Google recommends so that mail the chapter sends is not
marked as spam. The admin console lists them.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** send a message from a personal account outside the
chapter to the first administrator's address. It arrives.

**What goes wrong:** skipping the records that stop the chapter's own mail being
marked as spam. Everything works, and the software's welcome emails to new mentors
land in spam folders. Step 14.4 already warns about this.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 4.5 Create the chapter's own administrator account

**Done when:** a named person at the chapter holds a Google Workspace administrator
account on a chapter address.

**Who:** the chapter.

**First:** step 4.2.

**How to do it today:** the first administrator from step 4.2 is this account. Give
it the highest administrator level Google Workspace offers. A second person at the
chapter should hold the same level too, so that one person leaving does not lock
the chapter out.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the named person signs in to the admin console with their
chapter address.

**What goes wrong:** the chapter's only administrator being the central support
organization. That breaks the settled rule that the chapter can always withdraw the
central support organization's access.

**Status:** not yet tried.

---

### 4.6 Create the central support organization's administrator account

**Done when:** the central support organization holds its own named administrator
account, separate from the chapter's.

**Who:** the chapter's administrator creates it; the central support organization
uses it.

**First:** step 4.5, and the access agreed in the agreement (step 2.4).

**How to do it today:** the chapter's administrator creates a user for the central
support organization, gives it administrator rights, and hands over its sign-in
details privately. The central support organization's administrator enters the
software's permission grant later, in step 10.3, so it needs the highest
administrator level.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the central support organization signs in to the
chapter's admin console with its own account.

**What goes wrong:** the account uses a paid licence, like any user. Budget for it in
the cost agreement (step 2.2).

**Status:** not yet tried.

### 4.7 Create the shared operations mailbox

**Done when:** the shared address the software reads and sends from exists as a real
licensed mailbox, not as an alias or a group. An alias will not work.

**Who:** the chapter's administrator.

**First:** step 4.5.

**How to do it today:** create a user in Google Workspace for the shared address,
usually `info@` the chapter's email domain name, with its own licence. Staff read and
answer the chapter's public email through the software, and the software sends as
this address, so every staff member sees the same conversation.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the address appears in the admin console as a user with a
licence, not in the list of groups.

**What goes wrong:** creating the address as a group or as a second name for someone
else's mailbox. The software's machine account can only act as a real mailbox. It
fails later with an error that does not name the cause. Cleveland learned this when
its administrator address turned out to be a group. A second known failure: two
deployments reading the same mailbox capture every message twice. Only one
deployment may ever read a given shared operations mailbox.

**Status:** not yet tried. The rule is proven on Cleveland.

### 4.8 Create the alert sending mailbox

**Done when:** the address the software sends its warning messages from exists as a
real licensed mailbox. An alias or a group will be refused.

**Who:** the chapter's administrator.

**First:** step 4.5.

**How to do it today:** either create a separate licensed user for the address, or
use the shared operations mailbox from step 4.7. Cleveland uses its shared operations
mailbox for both, which saves a licence.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the address appears in the admin console as a user with a
licence.

**What goes wrong:** the same as step 4.7: a group or an alias is refused.

**Status:** not yet tried.

### 4.9 Decide who receives the system's warning messages

**Done when:** the receiving address is decided and it reaches a person who reads it
during the working week, not an unattended mailbox.

**Who:** the chapter and the central support organization together.

**First:** step 4.5.

**How to do it today:** the receiving address, unlike the sending one, may be a group.
Cleveland's hosting alerts go to one address that forwards every message to the named
system administrators. Copy that arrangement: one address, forwarded to named people
at the chapter and at the central support organization.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the named people are written down beside the address.
The real test is step 12.4, once the software can send.

**What goes wrong:** an address nobody reads. The warnings then arrive and change
nothing.

**Status:** not yet tried.

### 4.10 Create the members group

**Done when:** the group exists at its own address and the administrator can add and

**Who:** the chapter's administrator.

**First:** step 4.5.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the group appears in the admin console, and the

**What goes wrong:** adding the software's machine account to the group needs one more

### 4.11 Create the staff mailboxes

**Done when:** every member of staff who will use the system has a mailbox on the
chapter's domain. Mentors' mailboxes are created later, from their mentor records
(step 15.9).

**Who:** the chapter's administrator.

**First:** step 4.5.

**How to do it today:** create a licensed user for each member of staff, first name
and last name at the chapter's email domain name. Do not create mentors' mailboxes
here. When the Google connection is switched on, the software creates a mentor's
mailbox itself, from their mentor record, when the mentor reaches the
Accepted-Provisional status. That is part of creating the mentor accounts
(step 15.9). A mailbox created by hand first can make the software pick a different
address for that mentor.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** every name on the staff list (step 14.1) has a mailbox.

**What goes wrong:** using a staff member's personal address in the software. Members
of the chapter are addressed at their chapter address only. A personal address causes
duplicate calendar invitations.

**Status:** not yet tried.

### 4.12 Require two-step sign-in

**Done when:** two-step sign-in is enforced for every account, and recovery is possible
without one person.

**Who:** the chapter's administrator.

**First:** steps 4.5 to 4.11.

**How to do it today:** in the admin console, turn on two-step sign-in and then
enforce it for the whole organization, giving users a short grace period to set it
up. The shared mailboxes need a second step too. Store their recovery codes where two
people can reach them.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the admin console reports two-step sign-in as enforced,
and each user shows as enrolled.

**What goes wrong:** a shared mailbox whose second step is one person's phone. When
that person is away, nobody can sign in to it.

**Status:** not yet tried.

**Done when:** the application has been submitted with the chapter's nonprofit

**Who:** the chapter.

### 4.14 Move control of the registrar account to a chapter mailbox

**Done when:** the registrar account's contact address is a chapter mailbox, the
founder's personal address has been removed, and a password reset test lands in the
chapter mailbox.

**Who:** the chapter's setup contact.

**First:** step 4.4.

**How to do it today:** in the registrar account, change the contact and sign-in
address to a chapter mailbox that more than one person can reach. The shared
operations mailbox from step 4.7 is one choice. Remove the founding email address
from step 3.1. Then ask the registrar for a password reset, and check the message
arrives in the chapter mailbox.

**How it will be done later:** unchanged.

**How you know it worked:** the password reset message arrives in the chapter mailbox.

**What goes wrong:** a loop. If the chapter's email is ever down because of a domain
name problem, the password reset for the registrar account goes to that same broken
email. Keep the registrar account's recovery codes from step 3.6 safe for exactly
this case.

**Status:** not yet tried.

## What writing these steps found

**1. The agreement was in draft.** When these steps were written, stage 2 could only
say what the agreement must contain. Doug confirmed on 09-18-26 that a draft exists
and is in review (work list item 7). Its final cost may still depend on two things
not yet ruled: whether the fee covers labour only (proposed, not
ruled), and whether the two paid CRM add-on products are part of the standard (an
open question), because each changes the cost.

**2. Mentor mailboxes are created by the software, not by hand.** Step 4.11 says
"every person who will use the system has a mailbox". Mentors are people who use the
system, but their mailboxes should come from Mentor Administration, as their accounts
now do (step 15.9). **Recommended step-list correction:** step 4.11's "Done when"
should read "every member of staff who will use the system has a mailbox on the
chapter's domain. Mentors' mailboxes are created later, from their mentor records
(step 15.9)." This document keeps the step list's wording and explains it in the
method.

**3. The second Google Workspace branch has no written steps.** Stage 4 is written for
a chapter that holds its own Google Workspace, which is the proposed default. A
chapter given one by the central support organization would follow different steps,
and none are written. The proposal to prefer the first branch is still unruled.

**4. The receiving address for warnings may be a group.** Step 4.9 is consistent with
step 12.4: sending must be from a real mailbox, receiving may be a group forwarded to
named people. No step-list change is needed, but the two steps should stay in
agreement.
